Protective Programs

A physical security assessment checklist for government agencies

A physical security assessment is only useful if it produces decisions, not a binder. Here is what a rigorous assessment evaluates, in the order it should be evaluated, and what the deliverable should let a program office actually do.

Apex Gama Defense Solutions · 9 min read

Most physical security assessments fail in one of two ways. They either walk a facility with a generic checklist and grade it against controls no one tied to a real threat, or they produce a document so long that nobody acts on it. A useful assessment does the opposite: it starts from what has to be protected, works outward through concentric layers of protection, and ends with a short, prioritized list of decisions a program office can fund and defend. The checklist below is the structure we use, in sequence.

1. Start with the asset and the threat, not the camera

Before anyone looks at a lock, the assessment has to answer two questions: what are we protecting, and from whom. Identify the critical assets — people, specific spaces, information, operations whose disruption carries a public consequence — and rank them by the impact of their loss or compromise. Then define the threats that are credible for this facility and location: the design-basis threat, if the agency has one, plus the realistic range of insider, criminal, and disruptive actors. Every control that follows is justified by this step. A camera that watches a door no adversary would use is a cost, not a control.

2. Map the concentric layers of protection

Effective physical security is layered — deter, detect, delay, respond — from the property boundary inward to the asset. The assessment should walk those layers explicitly: the site perimeter, the building envelope, the interior spaces, and the specific rooms or assets at the center. For each layer, the question is the same: does it deter, does it detect an intrusion, how long does it delay an adversary, and does that delay exceed the time it takes the response force to arrive? When delay is shorter than response time, the layer is decorative.

3. Perimeter, standoff, and approach

  • Property boundary definition, fencing or natural barriers, and gaps
  • Vehicle approach, standoff distance, and anti-ram measures where the threat warrants them
  • Parking placement relative to the building and to critical assets
  • Clear zones, sightlines, and anything that gives an adversary concealment
  • Signage establishing the boundary and the rules for entry

4. Access control and credentialing

Access control is where most findings cluster, because it is where policy and technology meet daily human behavior. Evaluate how identities are vetted and credentials issued, how access rights are assigned and — more importantly — revoked when someone leaves or changes roles, and whether the system enforces least privilege. Check the physical points: are doors that should be controlled actually controlled, are they defeated by propping or tailgating, and do alarms on forced or held-open doors get a response or get ignored. A credential system is only as strong as its worst-maintained door.

5. Screening and visitor management

Assess how visitors, vendors, and deliveries are received, screened, escorted, and tracked. Look at where screening happens relative to the assets — screening that occurs after a visitor is already past the critical boundary is misplaced. Mail and delivery handling, loading docks, and contractor access are frequent blind spots and deserve their own line items.

6. Surveillance, detection, and alarms

Cameras and sensors are worth exactly as much as the monitoring and response behind them. Evaluate coverage against the layers and assets that matter, image quality and retention, and — the real test — whether anyone is watching, and what happens when a sensor trips. Intrusion detection, duress alarms, and door alarms should each map to a defined response. An alarm with no response is a liability, because it documents that the organization knew and did nothing.

7. Security force and response

Assess the response concept: who responds, how fast, with what authority, and under what post orders. The assessment should test response time against the delay the layers provide, and confirm that procedures — lockdown, escalation, law-enforcement notification — are written and rehearsed, not assumed. Note that assessment and design of this concept is advisory work; the guard force itself is a licensed service delivered by qualified providers, and a good assessment keeps that line clear.

8. Locks, keys, and the quiet failures

  • Key control: who holds keys, how they are tracked, and when they were last re-keyed
  • Lock hardware condition and whether it matches the door's security rating
  • Lighting for deterrence and for usable camera images at night
  • Landscaping and natural surveillance (CPTED principles)
  • Signage, wayfinding, and anything that reveals the location of critical assets

9. Procedural and information controls

Physical and information security are not separable. The assessment should cover how sensitive information is stored and destroyed, clean-desk and visitor-escort practices, and the insider-risk indicators that physical controls are meant to catch. Procedures are controls too, and the cheapest ones to fix.

10. The deliverable: a prioritized findings matrix

The output is not a narrative; it is a decision tool. Each finding should state the vulnerability, the asset and threat it exposes, the likelihood and impact, and a specific, costed recommendation with an owner and a priority. Group the recommendations into what can be fixed immediately at low cost (procedure and discipline), what needs a modest project (hardware and configuration), and what requires capital planning (construction and standoff). That structure lets a program office act on the top of the list on Monday and build the rest into a budget cycle — which is the whole point of commissioning the assessment.

Turning the assessment into a program

A one-time assessment is a snapshot. The facilities that stay secure treat it as the first iteration of a protective program: the findings feed a governance structure, the recommendations become tracked projects with owners, and the whole thing is re-assessed on a cadence as the threat, the mission, and the building change. The assessment earns its cost when it starts that loop.

This article describes general physical-security assessment practice. It is not a statement of any specific agency standard or regulatory requirement, and it does not describe a particular engagement. The applicable standard for any assessment is set by the customer and the solicitation.
